# Evidence

Finding: The relative increase in all-cause mortality per unit of BMI above 25 is larger in younger than older adults: hazard ratio 1.52 per 5 kg/m² when BMI is measured at 35–49, against 1.21 at 70–89; the BMI with the lowest death rate rises with age.

Type: moderator.

Source: Global BMI Mortality Collaboration (Di Angelantonio E, Bhupathiraju ShN, Wormser D, et al.). Body-mass index and all-cause mortality: individual-participant-data meta-analysis of 239 prospective studies in four continents. Lancet 388(10046), 776–786 (2016). doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30175-1. PubMed 27423262. Full text (open access): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4995441/. Individual data not publicly released.

Design: Individual-participant-data meta-analysis. 10,625,411 participants in 239 prospective studies (Asia, Australia and New Zealand, Europe, North America), median follow-up 13.7 years. Primary analyses restricted to 3,951,455 never-smokers in 189 studies without chronic disease at recruitment who survived 5 years, with 385,879 deaths; hazard ratios adjusted for study, age and sex.

Key numbers:
- HR per 5 kg/m² above BMI 25: 1.52 (1.47–1.56) at baseline age 35–49; 1.21 (1.17–1.25) at 70–89 (trend p < 0.0001); men 1.51 (1.46–1.56), women 1.30 (1.26–1.33); overall 1.31 (1.29–1.33); Europe 1.39 (1.34–1.43), North America 1.29 (1.26–1.32), east Asia 1.39 (1.34–1.44), Australia and New Zealand 1.31 (1.27–1.35). Abstract and Results. Transcribed and asserted.
- "52% higher" and "21%": HR − 1, derived.
- BMI with lowest mortality: 22 at 35–49, 23 at 50–69, 24 at 70–89. Results. Transcribed and asserted.
- HR by BMI band versus 22.5–25: 1.51, 1.13, 1.00, 1.00, 1.07, 1.20, 1.45, 1.94, 2.76, with the paper's floating-variance 95% CIs (which is why the reference band has a CI). Abstract and Results. Chart only.
- 2.0 million: Bhaskaran's never-smoker analysis (1,969,648 people, 188,057 deaths), from which the age finding comes; the full study had 3.6 million. Abstract.

Corroboration:
- Bhaskaran K, dos-Santos-Silva I, Leon DA, Douglas IJ, Smeeth L. Association of BMI with overall and cause-specific mortality: a population-based cohort study of 3·6 million adults in the UK. Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 6(12), 944–953 (2018). doi:10.1016/S2213-8587(18)30288-2. UK primary-care records (not part of the collaboration's cohorts): "Associations between BMI and mortality were stronger at younger ages than at older ages, and the BMI associated with lowest mortality risk was higher in older individuals."
- Winter JE, MacInnis RJ, Nowson CA. The influence of age on the BMI and all-cause mortality association: a meta-analysis. J Nutr Health Aging 21(10), 1254–1258 (2017). doi:10.1007/s12603-016-0837-4. Eight cohorts spanning all adult ages (370,416 people): in those 65 and over mortality rose at BMI below 22, not seen in younger adults, and the lowest-risk range was higher in older adults. Smaller, and the first author is a Nestlé Health Science employee.
- Stevens J, Cai J, Pamuk ER, Williamson DF, Thun MJ, Wood JL. The effect of age on the association between body-mass index and mortality. N Engl J Med 338(1), 1–7 (1998). doi:10.1056/NEJM199801013380101. 324,135 never-smokers in the American Cancer Society's CPS-I; "the relative risk associated with greater body-mass index declined with age". The collaboration includes American Cancer Society cohorts, so this may overlap with its data; Bhaskaran and Winter are the independent corroboration.
- Aune D, Sen A, Prasad M, et al. BMI and all cause mortality: systematic review and non-linear dose-response meta-analysis of 230 cohort studies. BMJ 353, i2156 (2016). doi:10.1136/bmj.i2156. J-shaped curve with lowest risk at BMI 22–24 in never-smokers (supports panel 2, not the age pattern directly).

Wording check: Observational, so "goes with", "went with" and "link" throughout; no claim that weight causes death or that losing weight changes risk by these amounts.

Limits: The collaboration's study selection and its estimates for the overweight range (BMI 25–30) were criticised (Flegal and Ioannidis, J Clin Epidemiol 2017); the post's claim is the age pattern, which those critiques do not address and which independent data show. Relative risks are smaller at older ages but absolute death rates are much higher, so the absolute excess can still be large. BMI is a crude measure of body fat, and the 50–69 per-5-unit estimate is only in the appendix, so the chart shows the two end groups.

Fact-check: An independent subagent confirmed every number against the full text (PMC4995441) and PubMed, confirmed the adjustment and baseline-age wording, confirmed Bhaskaran 2018, Stevens 1998 and Aune 2016 say what is claimed, and found that the 2017 critiques (Flegal and Ioannidis; Lancet letters) target study selection and the overweight range, not the age pattern; no retraction or correction. Verdict: pass with fixes. Changed as a result: the summary now says 'when BMI was measured'; the UK study is described by its 2.0 million never-smoker analysis; panel 2 notes floating-variance CIs and its log axis; Stevens 1998 is flagged as possibly overlapping with the collaboration's data and Winter et al. 2017 added as a second independent source; 'without chronic disease' wording matched to the paper.
