# Evidence

Finding: In children, lower IQ goes with higher blood lead along a curve that is steepest at low concentrations: 6.7 IQ points from 2.4 to 30 µg/dL, of which 3.8 between 2.4 and 10.

Type: dose-response.

Source: Lanphear BP, Hornung R, Khoury J, Yolton K, Baghurst P, Bellinger DC, et al. Low-level environmental lead exposure and children's intellectual function: an international pooled analysis. Environ Health Perspect 113(7), 894–899 (2005). doi:10.1289/ehp.7688. PubMed 16002379. Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1257652/. Erratum with the complete corrected article: Environ Health Perspect 127(9), 099001 (2019), doi:10.1289/EHP5685, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6792371/. The erratum corrected Boston-cohort data (performance IQ used instead of full-scale IQ; blood lead conversion error); the US EPA and others judged the effect on the results minor. All numbers here are the corrected ones.

Design: Pooled analysis of seven prospective birth or infancy cohorts (Boston, Cincinnati, Cleveland, Rochester, Mexico City, Port Pirie, Yugoslavia), 1,333 children followed from birth or infancy, full-scale IQ tested between 4 years 10 months and 7 years (Boston at 10) as outcome, concurrent blood lead as exposure, adjusted for site, maternal IQ, HOME score, maternal education and birth weight. The log-linear form was chosen after a restricted cubic spline showed significant non-linear terms (quadratic p < 0.001, cubic p = 0.003), and the slope was steeper for children whose maximal blood lead was below 7.5 µg/dL (p = 0.02); that, not the step arithmetic, is the evidence for the shape.

Key numbers:
- 6.7 IQ points (95% CI 4.1–9.3) lower from 2.4 to 30 µg/dL. Corrected abstract (2019 erratum). Transcribed and asserted.
- Step estimates: 3.8 (2.3–5.3) from 2.4 to 10; 1.8 (1.1–2.6) from 10 to 20; 1.1 (0.7–1.5) from 20 to 30. Corrected abstract. Transcribed and asserted; `analysis.py` recovers a single log-linear slope (2.65 IQ points per natural-log unit) from the 6.7 estimate and asserts it reproduces all three step estimates to one decimal.
- "More than half": 3.8 / 6.7 = 57%, derived.
- Per microgram: 0.50 (0.30–0.70), 0.18 (0.11–0.26) and 0.11 (0.07–0.15) IQ points per µg/dL (step estimate and CI ÷ step width), derived here. "More than four times": 4.5 from the rounded estimates, 4.6 in the model; this ratio follows from the log-linear form and has no separate uncertainty.
- Chart band: 95% CI of the slope, derived from the 4.1–9.3 interval by the same scaling.

Corroboration:
- US EPA ISA 2024 on shape: "Several epidemiologic studies observed a supralinear C-R relationship (i.e., larger decrement in cognitive function per unit increase in blood Pb level in children in the lower range...)". This corroborates the shape from data beyond the pooled analysis.
- Crump KS, Van Landingham C, Bowers TS, Cahoy D, Chandalia JK. A statistical reevaluation of the data used in the Lanphear et al. (2005) pooled-analysis. Crit Rev Toxicol 43(9), 785–799 (2013). doi:10.3109/10408444.2013.832726. Independent reanalysis (by authors including a critic of the original) found the exposure-response non-linear over the full range, with a larger IQ decrement per unit at lower blood lead, and an association at maximal blood lead ≤7 µg/dL. It also found that below 10 µg/dL the curve is adequately modelled as linear (same data, so not independent).
- US Environmental Protection Agency. Integrated Science Assessment for Lead (Final Report, 2024), EPA/600/R-23/375. https://www.epa.gov/isa/integrated-science-assessment-isa-lead. Consensus assessment concluding a causal relationship between lead exposure and cognitive effects in children, with evidence at low blood lead levels. (Its 2013 predecessor also reviewed the Lanphear data errors.)
- National Toxicology Program. NTP Monograph on Health Effects of Low-Level Lead (2012). https://ntp.niehs.nih.gov/go/mgraph01. Sufficient evidence that blood lead below 5 µg/dL is associated with decreased IQ and academic achievement in children.
- Budtz-Jørgensen E, Bellinger D, Lanphear B, Grandjean P. An international pooled analysis for obtaining a benchmark dose for environmental lead exposure in children. Risk Anal 33(3), 450–461 (2013). doi:10.1111/j.1539-6924.2012.01882.x. Same data, different methods: supra-linear curve, limited variation between studies (not independent data).

Wording check: The cohorts are observational, so the post uses "was lower", "scored lower", "gap" and "difference" to describe between-child differences, not "causes" or "loss". The Evidence line notes the EPA's causal judgement for lead and cognition, which rests on toxicology and many studies, not on this curve alone.

Limits: Few children had low maximal blood lead (8.9% below 7.5 µg/dL), so the low end of the curve is the least certain (in the original data, 67% of children below 7.5 µg/dL came from Rochester; the authors reported that dropping Rochester changed the coefficient by less than 3%); Bowers and Beck (2006) argued that a supra-linear shape can arise partly from lognormal exposure distributions, though the Crump reanalysis still found non-linearity. Residual confounding (for example by socioeconomic factors) cannot be ruled out. Blood lead levels in these 1980s–1990s cohorts were far higher than in children today.

Fact-check: An independent subagent checked every number against the corrected article (PMC6792371), confirmed the cohorts, covariates and 8.9% below 7.5 µg/dL, confirmed Crump 2013, the EPA ISA 2024 (causal relationship and supralinear concentration-response), NTP 2012 and Budtz-Jørgensen 2013, and found no retraction or overturning dispute beyond the 2019 erratum. Verdict: pass with fixes.
