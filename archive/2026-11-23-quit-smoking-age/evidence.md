# Evidence

Finding: The years of life regained by quitting smoking depend on the age at quitting: about 10 years at 30, 9 at 40, 6 at 50 and 3 at 60, compared with continuing smokers.

Type: moderator.

Source: Doll R, Peto R, Boreham J, Sutherland I. Mortality in relation to smoking: 50 years' observations on male British doctors. BMJ 328, 1519 (2004). doi:10.1136/bmj.38142.554479.AE. PubMed 15213107. Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC437139/. No released data.

Design: Prospective cohort of 34,439 male British doctors, smoking habits recorded from 1951 and periodically after, cause-specific mortality followed for 50 years (1951–2001). Panel 2: Million Women Study, 1.3 million UK women recruited 1996–2001, 1.2 million after excluding prior disease, followed about 12 years through national mortality records (66,489 deaths), Cox models comparing ex-smokers by age at stopping with otherwise similar never-smokers.

Key numbers:
- Continuing cigarette smokers (men born 1900–1930) died about 10 years younger than lifelong non-smokers; cessation at 60, 50, 40 or 30 gained about 3, 6, 9 or 10 years. Doll 2004, abstract (Results). Transcribed; `analysis.py` asserts the sentences appear in the saved abstract.
- US adults: quitting at 25–34, 35–44, 45–54 gained about 10, 9 and 6 years. Jha 2013, abstract (Results). Transcribed and asserted.
- UK women, all-cause relative risk vs never-smokers: stopped at 25–34, 1.05 (1.00–1.11); 35–44, 1.20 (1.14–1.26); 45–54, 1.56 (1.49–1.64); still smoking at the 3-year resurvey, 2.97 (2.88–3.07). Pirie 2013, abstract (Findings) and Results (PMC full text). Transcribed and asserted.
- Excess risk avoided: 97% (25–34) and 90% (35–44). Derived here as 1 − (RR − 1)/(2.97 − 1); `analysis.py` asserts the remaining excess rounds to the paper's 3% and 10%. 72% for 45–54 is derived here (chart only); the paper says "at least two-thirds" using the upper confidence limit.
- 34,439 doctors; 1.2 million women. Abstracts.

Corroboration:
- Jha P, Ramasundarahettige C, Landsman V, et al. 21st-century hazards of smoking and benefits of cessation in the United States. NEJM 368, 341–350 (2013). doi:10.1056/NEJMsa1211128. US National Health Interview Survey, 202,248 adults; quitting at 25–34, 35–44, 45–54 gained about 10, 9 and 6 years; quitting before 40 cuts the excess risk by about 90%.
- Pirie K, Peto R, Reeves GK, Green J, Beral V. The 21st century hazards of smoking and benefits of stopping: a prospective study of one million women in the UK. Lancet 381, 133–141 (2013). doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(12)61720-6. Stopping before 40 avoids more than 90% of the excess mortality of continuing smokers; before 30, more than 97%.
- Cho ER, Brill IK, Gram IT, Brown PE, Jha P. Smoking cessation and short- and longer-term mortality. NEJM Evidence 3(3), EVIDoa2300272 (2024). doi:10.1056/EVIDoa2300272. Four national cohorts (US, UK, Norway, Canada), 1.48 million adults; cessation at every age linked to longer survival, particularly before 40.
- US Department of Health and Human Services. Smoking Cessation: A Report of the Surgeon General (2020). https://www.hhs.gov/surgeongeneral/reports-and-publications/tobacco/2020-cessation-sgr-factsheet-key-findings/. Key finding: smoking cessation reduces the risk of premature death and can add as much as a decade to life expectancy.

Wording check: The data are observational cohorts. "Gained" and "avoided" are the papers' own terms. Causal language about quitting is supported by an accepted mechanism with a replicated dose-response (smoking causes the diseases that make up the excess deaths, per IARC and the Surgeon General) and by the Surgeon General's 2020 causal conclusion that cessation reduces premature death. The headline's "gain" (over continuing smokers) rests on that consensus; the post reports the specific magnitudes as measured in each cohort rather than as guaranteed individual gains.

Limits: Quitters can differ from continuing smokers (health, education, motivation); the studies adjust for age and some confounders and Pirie excludes women with prior disease, but residual selection remains, and one selection-corrected economic model (Darden et al., International Economic Review 2018) estimates smaller gains. The doctors' cohort is men born 1900–1930, and the years gained are approximate figures without intervals. The Million Women Study measures risk in later middle age over about 12 years, not lifetime.

Fact-check: An independent subagent checked every number against the PubMed abstracts and the PMC full text of Pirie et al. (34,439 doctors; 3, 6, 9, 10 years; Jha's 10, 9, 6; relative risks 1.05, 1.20, 1.56 and 2.97 with intervals; 1.2 million women), recomputed 97%, 90% and 72%, confirmed all four DOIs on Crossref with no correction or retraction notices, confirmed the Surgeon General 2020 key finding, and judged Darden et al. 2018 a minority selection critique already covered under Limits.
