# Evidence

Finding: Programmes that get children outdoors more (mostly at school) lower the share who develop myopia; in pooled randomised trials over two years, 22.5% against 26.7% (risk ratio 0.84).

Type: effect.

Source: Kido A, Miyake M, Watanabe N. Interventions to increase time spent outdoors for preventing incidence and progression of myopia in children. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2024, Issue 6, CD013549. doi:10.1002/14651858.CD013549.pub2. Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11167692/ (PMID 38864362). No individual-level data released.

Design: Systematic review and meta-analysis of five randomised trials (four cluster-randomised by school; interventions ranged from outdoor classes and recess routines to a parent-education campaign), 10,733 primary school children, mostly aged 6 to 9, all in Asia; searches to June 2022; GRADE certainty assessed. The largest component trials: He M et al., JAMA 2015 (12 Guangzhou schools, 1,903 children, 3 years) and He X et al., Ophthalmology 2022 (24 Shanghai schools, 6,295 children, 2 years, two intervention arms).

Key numbers:
- Pooled incidence with vs without extra outdoor time: 7.1% vs 9.5% at 1 year (RR 0.82, 0.56–1.19; 3 studies, 1,265 participants; low certainty), 22.5% vs 26.7% at 2 years (RR 0.84, 0.72–0.98; 3 studies, 2,104; moderate), 30.5% vs 39.8% at 3 years (RR 0.77, 0.59–1.01; 1 study, 394; moderate). The 3-year row is He 2015 alone, scaled down for clustering; the chart says so. Kido 2024, abstract, Main results. Transcribed; `analysis.py` asserts each string appears in the saved abstract.
- Five RCTs, 10,733 participants. Kido 2024, abstract. Transcribed.
- Guangzhou: 259 of 853 (30.4%) vs 287 of 726 (39.5%) over 3 years; difference -9.1 points (95% CI -14.1 to -4.1); 12 schools; extra 40-minute outdoor class each school day; children aged about 6. He 2015, abstract. Percentages reproduced from the counts in `analysis.py` (raw difference -9.17 points; paper reports -9.1).
- Shanghai: 2-year incidence 24.9% (control), 20.6% (+40 min), 23.8% (+80 min); adjusted IRR 0.84 (0.72–0.99) and 0.89 (0.79–0.99). He 2022, abstract. Transcribed; chart only.
- Cao 2020: pooled RR 0.76 (0.67–0.87), five RCTs. Abstract. Transcribed.

Corroboration:
- Cao K, Wan Y, Yusufu M, Wang N. Significance of outdoor time for myopia prevention: a systematic review and meta-analysis based on randomized controlled trials. Ophthalmic Research 63, 97–105 (2020). doi:10.1159/000501937. Five RCTs, 3,014 children, overlapping Cochrane's trials (He 2015 is in both); new myopia cases fewer with outdoor time, RR 0.76 (0.67–0.87), no heterogeneity.
- Xiong S et al. Time spent in outdoor activities in relation to myopia prevention and control: a meta-analysis and systematic review. Acta Ophthalmologica 95, 551–566 (2017). doi:10.1111/aos.13403. Clinical trials RR 0.536 (0.338–0.850) and cohort studies RR 0.574 for incident myopia; no effect on progression in eyes already myopic.
- He M et al. Effect of time spent outdoors at school on the development of myopia among children in China: a randomized clinical trial. JAMA 314, 1142–1148 (2015). doi:10.1001/jama.2015.10803. The Guangzhou result above.
- International Myopia Institute. IMI 2025 Digest. https://myopiainstitute.org/imi-whitepaper/imi-2025-digest/. Calls time outdoors the most reliable protective factor, supported by observational evidence and RCTs, chiefly for delaying onset.

Wording check: "Lowers" and "cut" are causal verbs. They fit: the evidence is randomised trials, pooled in a Cochrane review. The claim is limited to onset (new cases). The post flags that the effect on myopia already under way is less certain, because Cochrane rates progression evidence low certainty and Xiong et al. found no effect in myopes.

Limits: All trials were in East Asia, where myopia is common; Cochrane says generalisability elsewhere is unknown. The one-year and three-year pooled intervals include no effect, and the three-year estimate rests on one trial. The Shanghai 80-minute arm did no better than the 40-minute arm, probably because measured outdoor time was similar in both.

Fact-check: An independent subagent confirmed every number against the Cochrane, He 2015, He 2022, Cao 2020 and Xiong 2017 abstracts and the Cochrane full text, confirmed the DOIs and the IMI digest wording, and found no retraction, erratum or credible dispute. Verdict: pass with fixes. Changed as a result: the pooled trials include non-school programmes (a parent-education campaign), so the headline, summary, body and chart now say 'programmes to get children outdoors' rather than 'extra outdoor time at school'; the chart and alt text now say the three-year pooled row is the Guangzhou trial alone; 'each day' corrected to 'each school day'; Cao 2020 noted as overlapping Cochrane's trials.
